Credible: a bank loan predictor with reduced bias for improved profit 

Veritas AI International High School Hackathon 2026 · TEAM UNREAL MADRID SUBMISSION 

Track: AI + Finance (built on suggested project 2.3, Fairness audit of a loan model) Team: Unreal Madrid; Rayaan Vakas and Riyen Shah 

Credible helps banks make fair and cost effective decisions when deciding whether loan applicants are good or bad risks, while minimising bias and losses. 

Disclaimer: This project is a prototype and is not financial advice 

We used the Statlog (German Credit Data) dataset (Hans Hofmann, UC Irvine Machine Learning Repository): 1,000 real loan applications, each containing 20 columns of information about the applicant, and whether they were a good or bad risk for the bank to provide a loan. We cleaned and re-encoded the data to make it easily accessible by our artificial intelligence model and printed the amount of people who were deemed "Good" (1) for a loan, and those deemed "Bad" (0) for a loan. Next, we split our data so the model trains using a randomly generated 80% of it and is tested on the remaining 20%. We tested model 1 and compared it against our baseline and saw a 1.5% improvement in accuracy; however, we knew the model could be more accurate. So, we coded a csv file formed of all the records that the model had incorrectly predicted and opened it as an excel spreadsheet. We then analysed the data, finding that there were two distinct groups with a particularly high error rate. These groups were people aged under 30, and people with a loan duration between 13-24 months. We then referred back to a spreadsheet we had created of all 1000 applicants, and found that a whopping 411 people fell into each group (411 people under 30yrs, 411 people with loans from 13-24 months). Of the 411 people aged under 30 around 60% were good risks, and of the 411 people with loans between 13-24 months over 60% were good risks. This explains why out of the incorrect predictions in each group, most were bad risks being accepted compared to good risks being rejected, between the model has trained on a dataset where majority of those groups are good risks, which created a bias in its predictions. This would also prove very dangerous for a bank, since an accepted bad risk loan costs 5x more than a denied good risk loan. We countered the bias by using a weight coefficient to give more weight to the bad risk applicants in the "problem groups". Essentially, we multiplied the number of bad applicants in the groups by a set constant to improve how seriously our model treats them in the test data, hence countering bias. In order to determine this constant, we created model 2. which looped through our model predictions, each time with a different constant 1, 2, 3, 5 (where 1 is just the original proportion/results). After comparing the percentages of bad loans approved and good loans rejected with each coefficient. We found that the coefficient 3 had the lowest "bad accepted" percentage without a "good rejected" percentage exceeding 50%. Now that we had discovered our weight coefficient, we created model 3. this model implemented our weight coefficient to counteract the bias within our problem groups. After running model 3, we compared our results to model 1, and found that the accuracy had decreased from 71.5% in model 1 to 68% in model 3 (2% below the baseline). We know the cost to the bank is 5 times worse when accepting a bad risk loan than rejecting a good risk loan, so we calculated how much money this model would cost the bank, and compared it to the baseline and model 1. We could see a drastic decrease in cost from this model as it prioritised rejecting bad risk loans, saving the bank more money. We compared cost by creating a bank cost rating for each model, which decreased from 185 in model 1 to 136 in model 3. This proved that our weight coefficient reduced cost to the bank despite the drop in accuracy. The percentage of bad risks accepted had significantly decreased. This demonstrated how we successfully managed to counter the bias we found in model 1 and create a model which proved much safer for banks to use. To further improve this model, we would allow for an adjustable weight coefficient where banks can decide the level of risks they want to take in order to increase accuracy and profits but endure potentially greater costs.
